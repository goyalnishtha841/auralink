The problem

When speech fails at 35,000 feet, nothing repairs it.

72

Mrs. Sharma, 72, is flying Delhi to Tokyo to see her son. She speaks soft Hinglish. The cabin is loud. She asks for “thoda wotter and kambal”. The crew member asks her to repeat, then guesses. After the second try she stops asking. Later a safety announcement plays in fast English. She nods. Nobody knows she did not understand.

01

The machine guesses once

Speech-to-text takes its first guess as final, even when it is unsure. “Kambal” becomes “camel”, and the request is lost.

02

“Please repeat” is the only fix

Saying it again, louder, is hardest for seniors, soft speakers and anyone with hearing or speech difficulty.

03

A nod is not understanding

Crew cannot tell which passengers actually understood a safety instruction, so they repeat it to everyone.

The real gap is repair: when meaning is lost, in either direction, nothing brings it back.

On board

Hear. Check. Deliver.

A passenger scans the seat QR and speaks. Auralink matches what it heard by how it sounds, checks with her in Hindi, and the crew get one clear card in polite Japanese. The reply comes back in her language.

How Auralink fixes it

Ask, don’t guess. Then make sure everyone understood.

  1. Say it any way

    Speak, tap a picture or type, in Hindi, Tamil, Thai, English or a mix. Scan the seat QR code; there is nothing to install.

  2. Listen for meaning

    Auralink compares what it heard with words people really use on board, like paani, kambal and dawai, and considers the moment, for example meal time.

  3. Ask the easiest question

    If it is not sure, it shows two big pictures to choose from. If it is still unsure, the crew get a “please help” card with its best guesses.

  4. One clear card for crew

    The crew see the request in polite Japanese and reply with one tap. The passenger reads the reply in their own language.

Urgent requests skip the queue. If it sounds like a medical problem, the crew are alerted straight away while the passenger confirms.

Flight phase

The other direction

Know who understood the safety briefing.

The crew send a pre-approved message. Each passenger sees pictures and a caption in their own language, then taps I understood or Explain again.

  • Understood
  • No answer yet
  • Asked to explain again

Instead of repeating the announcement to 54 people, the crew walk to the few seats that need help. At boarding, exit-row passengers get a picture check too, supporting the crew’s own confirmation.

Built for a real cabin

Simple for crew. Reliable at altitude.

No internet needed

A laptop-sized hub on board runs the speech engine. Phones connect over the cabin network. If the hub fails, crew simply carry on as they do today.

Scan and speak

The seat QR code opens Auralink in the browser, already set to that seat.

New language? Add words

Languages are word lists in a file. No retraining: Tamil and Thai were added this way.

One tap for crew

A card, an Acknowledge button and polite replies in Japanese.

No phone? No problem

A tactile seat panel with four raised buttons, or the in-seat screen, does the same job.

Dignity and privacy first

Large text for seniors, a picture-only mode for hearing or speech difficulty, gentle wording like “Let’s find it together”, and speech used only for the request, never stored.
